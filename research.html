Research

Dementia is one of the greatest health challenges of our ageing societies, yet the processes that drive the brain from healthy ageing into neurodegeneration remain poorly understood. My research asks how the brain's clearance, immune and organelle systems break down with age, and how these failures can be turned into targets for new medicines.

Research interests

  • Microglia
  • Neuroinflammation
  • Tau pathology
  • Glymphatic system
  • Brain ageing
  • Organelle biology
  • Drug discovery
  • Microglia and neuroinflammation – how microglia, astrocytes, peripheral immune cells and chronic, age-related inflammation ("inflammaging") contribute to neuronal damage, and how microglia and tau influence each other.
  • Tau pathology – the kinases and cellular stresses that drive abnormal tau, its accumulation and spread, and how these can be targeted pharmacologically.
  • Brain clearance – how the glymphatic system and other waste-clearance pathways remove toxic proteins such as amyloid-β and tau, and why they become less efficient with age.

Research experience

Since 2025 I have trained as an undergraduate research assistant in four laboratories in Japan, each approaching neurodegeneration from a different angle.

Haematopoietic stem cells and the origins of dementia

Tohoku University · Graduate School of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Laboratory of Pharmacology · Sep 2026 – present · Sendai, Japan

Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Nariko Arimura

Dementia is usually studied from inside the brain, but growing evidence suggests that blood cells and plasma components also shape brain ageing and cognitive decline. Blood-forming (haematopoietic) stem cells in the bone marrow change with age and give rise to immune cells that can interact with the brain. Building on the laboratory's work on blood-derived factors in dementia and on how glial cells and neurons communicate, this project explores how age-related changes in the haematopoietic system may lay the foundation for dementia.

Project: Unraveling the Foundation of Dementia Development through Hematopoietic Stem Cells

Related lab publications: Nakazawa S, … Arimura N. Nihon Yakurigaku Zasshi 2025 (review: blood cells and plasma components in dementia) [DOI] · Dewa K, Arimura N, … Hoshino M. Nat Commun 2024 (neuronal DSCAM and astrocytic GLAST in synapse formation) [DOI]

Organelle-contact abnormalities in neurodegenerative disease

Shimane University · Faculty of Medicine, Department of Life Science (Chemistry) · Jun – Aug 2026 · Izumo, Japan

Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Seiji Watanabe

In neurodegenerative diseases, abnormal protein accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation are thought to interact to drive neuronal death, but the molecular links between them remain unclear. One candidate hub is the mitochondria-associated membrane (MAM), the contact site between the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria, which regulates Ca2+ homeostasis, lipid metabolism, mitochondrial dynamics, autophagy and responses to proteostatic stress. Assoc. Prof. Watanabe and colleagues have shown that MAM disruption is a pathology shared across ALS-causing genes, and that MAM collapse impairs the TBK1-mediated proteostatic stress response. Because the MAM is thought to contain sub-domains with distinct composition and function, only some of which may be selectively damaged in disease, this project aimed to characterise how MAM-centred organelle contacts change in neurodegenerative disease models. Using the laboratory's established cell-culture systems and stored samples, it compared MAM formation, localisation and related molecules between disease models and controls, and related these changes to mitochondrial morphology, proteostatic stress responses and neuroinflammation.

Project: Investigation of novel organelle-contact abnormalities using neurodegenerative disease models

Ageing-induced senoinflammation in dementia

Institute of Science Tokyo · Graduate School of Medical and Dental Sciences, Department of Pharmacology · Jan – Feb 2026 · Tokyo, Japan

Supervisor: Prof. Tomoyuki Furuyashiki

Senescent cells accumulate with age and release inflammatory signals ("senoinflammation"), which are thought to contribute to cognitive decline and dementia. Prof. Furuyashiki's laboratory studies how ageing and chronic stress alter brain function through inflammatory and metabolic mechanisms – for example, showing that synaptic mitochondrial oxidative stress in the prefrontal cortex contributes to individual differences in age-related cognitive inflexibility in mice, and that innate immune molecules drive stress-induced neuroinflammation. This project investigated how ageing-induced senoinflammation contributes to the pathology of dementia, as part of a national Moonshot program aiming to visualise and control this process.

JST Moonshot Goal 7 (HIGUCHI Project) – Elucidation, visualisation and control of ageing-induced senoinflammation in dementia

The role of MARK4 in neuroinflammation

Tokyo Metropolitan University · Graduate School of Science, Laboratory of Molecular Neuroscience · Dec 2025 – Jan 2026 · Tokyo, Japan

Supervisor: Prof. Kanae Ando

MARK4 is a kinase genetically and pathologically linked to Alzheimer's disease that phosphorylates tau and promotes its accumulation and toxicity. Prof. Ando's laboratory has shown that deleting Mark4 in a tauopathy mouse model reduces pathological tau, neurodegeneration and astrogliosis and improves memory and survival; that MARK4 enhances stress-granule formation under oxidative stress, linking oxidative stress to tau accumulation; and that a cell-penetrating peptide can inhibit MARK4 allosterically and mitigate tau toxicity. Building on these findings, this project studied the role of MARK4 in neuroinflammation, contributing to efforts to develop MARK4 inhibitors as a new therapy for tauopathies. Supported by an Undergraduate Research Scholarship from Tokyo Metropolitan University.

Brain/MINDS 2.0 – Development of a novel therapy for tauopathy: a MARK4 inhibitor
