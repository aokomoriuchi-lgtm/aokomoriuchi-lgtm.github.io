Research

Dementia is one of the greatest health challenges of our ageing societies, yet the processes that drive the brain from healthy ageing into neurodegeneration remain poorly understood. My research asks how the brain's clearance, immune and organelle systems break down with age, and how these failures can be turned into targets for new medicines.

Research interests

  • Microglia
  • Neuroinflammation
  • Tau pathology
  • Glymphatic system
  • Brain ageing
  • Organelle biology
  • Drug discovery
  • Microglia and neuroinflammation – how microglia, astrocytes, peripheral immune cells and chronic, age-related inflammation ("inflammaging") contribute to neuronal damage, and how microglia and tau influence each other.
  • Tau pathology – the kinases and cellular stresses that drive abnormal tau, its accumulation and spread, and how these can be targeted pharmacologically.
  • Brain clearance – how the glymphatic system and other waste-clearance pathways remove toxic proteins such as amyloid-β and tau, and why they become less efficient with age.

Haematopoietic stem cells and the origins of dementia

Tohoku University · Graduate School of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Laboratory of Pharmacology · Sep 2026 – present · Sendai, Japan

Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Nariko Arimura

The earliest triggers of dementia are still unknown, but growing evidence suggests that bone-marrow and blood cells, and the molecules they secrete, influence brain function. Assoc. Prof. Arimura's laboratory asks whether changes in haematopoietic (blood-forming) stem cells can lay the foundation for dementia. People with Down syndrome almost all develop early-onset Alzheimer's disease, and the laboratory has reported that wild-type mice given bone marrow from a Down syndrome model show cognitive decline even though their brains are genetically normal. The current work aims to identify the plasma metabolites and exosomes derived from these blood cells that reach the brain, and to explore how bone-marrow transplantation from young to aged mice affects hippocampal neurogenesis and behaviour – with the long-term goal of new therapeutic targets. My placement is part of this research theme.

Organelle-contact abnormalities in neurodegenerative disease

Shimane University · Faculty of Medicine, Department of Life Science (Chemistry) · Jun – Aug 2026 · Izumo, Japan

Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Seiji Watanabe

In neurodegenerative diseases, abnormal protein accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation are thought to interact to drive neuronal death, but the molecular links between them remain unclear. One candidate hub is the mitochondria-associated membrane (MAM), the contact site between the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria, which regulates Ca2+ homeostasis, lipid metabolism, mitochondrial dynamics, autophagy and responses to proteostatic stress. Assoc. Prof. Watanabe and colleagues have shown that MAM disruption is a pathology shared across ALS-causing genes, and that MAM collapse impairs the TBK1-mediated proteostatic stress response. The laboratory is now asking which MAM sub-domains are selectively affected in disease, whether microglia – the central cells of the brain's inflammatory response – also show MAM abnormalities, and whether controlling the MAM can suppress neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration.

My placement project, Investigation of novel organelle-contact abnormalities using neurodegenerative disease models, was planned to compare MAM formation, localisation and related molecules between disease models and controls, using the laboratory's established cell-culture systems and stored samples, and to relate these features to mitochondrial morphology, proteostatic stress responses and neuroinflammation.

Ageing-induced senoinflammation in dementia

Institute of Science Tokyo · Graduate School of Medical and Dental Sciences, Department of Pharmacology · Jan – Feb 2026 · Tokyo, Japan

Supervisor: Prof. Tomoyuki Furuyashiki

Senescent cells accumulate with age and release inflammatory signals ("senoinflammation"), which are thought to contribute to cognitive decline and dementia. Prof. Furuyashiki's laboratory studies how ageing and chronic stress alter brain function through inflammation – using mouse models in which inflammation is driven by chronic stress or ageing to track how inflammation reshapes neural circuits across the whole brain, and linking these changes to immune markers in patients. Recent work showed that synaptic mitochondrial oxidative stress in the prefrontal cortex contributes to individual differences in age-related cognitive inflexibility in mice. The laboratory is part of the Moonshot Goal 7 Higuchi Project, which aims to detect and control dementia pathogenesis at an early stage based on the concept that glial pathology develops into senoinflammation. My placement focused on this theme.

Host-lab projects: AMED Moonshot Goal 7, Higuchi Project (PM: Dr Makoto Higuchi, QST; Prof. Furuyashiki is a project member) – Early detection and control of dementia pathogenesis based on the concept of glial pathology developing into senoinflammation · AMED Brain/MINDS 2.0 3B-05-R7, FY2025–2029 (PI: Prof. Furuyashiki) – Whole-brain neural-circuit changes originating from inflammation in psychiatric and neurological disorders

The role of MARK4 in neuroinflammation

Tokyo Metropolitan University · Graduate School of Science, Laboratory of Molecular Neuroscience · Dec 2025 – Jan 2026 · Tokyo, Japan

Supervisor: Prof. Kanae Ando

MARK4 is a kinase genetically and pathologically linked to Alzheimer's disease that phosphorylates tau and promotes its accumulation and toxicity. Prof. Ando's laboratory has shown that deleting Mark4 in a tauopathy mouse model reduces pathological tau, neurodegeneration and astrogliosis and improves memory and survival, and has developed a cell-penetrating peptide that inhibits MARK4 allosterically and mitigates tau toxicity – work aimed at drugs that target this enzyme. The laboratory also studies how neuronal and glial metabolism shape brain ageing; in a fly model of tau toxicity, increasing glucose uptake in glia suppressed neuroinflammation and tau-induced neuronal death. My placement focused on the role of MARK4 in neuroinflammation, supported by an Undergraduate Research Scholarship from Tokyo Metropolitan University.
