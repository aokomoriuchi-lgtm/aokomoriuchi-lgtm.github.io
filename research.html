Research

Dementia is one of the greatest health challenges of our ageing societies, yet the processes that drive the brain from healthy ageing into neurodegeneration remain poorly understood. My research asks how the brain's clearance, immune and organelle systems break down with age, how these failures are shaped by the rest of the body – especially the bone marrow and the immune cells it produces – and how they can be turned into targets for new medicines.

Research interests

  • Microglia
  • Neuroinflammation
  • Brain–bone marrow axis
  • Haematopoiesis
  • Tau pathology
  • Glymphatic system
  • Brain ageing
  • Organelle biology
  • Drug discovery
  • Microglia and neuroinflammation – how microglia, astrocytes, peripheral immune cells and chronic, age-related inflammation ("inflammaging") contribute to neuronal damage, and how microglia and tau influence each other.
  • Brain–bone marrow axis – how ageing and disease reshape haematopoiesis in the bone marrow (including the skull marrow next to the brain), how this changes the monocytes and other immune cells that reach the brain, and whether resetting this "immune cell factory" – for example by targeting chronic type I interferon signalling – could protect the brain. Key reading: Abellanas et al., Nature Neuroscience 2026 and the accompanying research briefing.
  • Tau pathology – the kinases and cellular stresses that drive abnormal tau, its accumulation and spread, and how these can be targeted pharmacologically.
  • Brain clearance – how the glymphatic system and other waste-clearance pathways remove toxic proteins such as amyloid-β and tau, and why they become less efficient with age.

Research experience

Since 2025 I have trained as an undergraduate research assistant in four laboratories in Japan, each approaching neurodegeneration from a different angle.

The grants and publications listed under each placement belong to the host laboratories. They are given as background to each research theme and are not my own awards or publications; some were published after my placement ended.

Haematopoietic stem cells and the origins of dementia

Tohoku University · Graduate School of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Laboratory of Pharmacology · Sep 2026 – present · Sendai, Japan

Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Nariko Arimura

The question. Could dementia begin, in part, in the bone marrow? The earliest triggers of the disease are still unknown, and the blood – its cells and everything they secrete – is emerging as a surprisingly strong influence on the brain.

The host lab. Assoc. Prof. Arimura's laboratory studies how changes in haematopoietic (blood-forming) stem cells shape brain function. People with Down syndrome almost all develop early-onset Alzheimer's disease, and the lab has found that wild-type mice given bone marrow from a Down syndrome model develop cognitive decline even though their brains are genetically normal. The team is now tracking the plasma metabolites and exosomes from these blood cells that reach the brain, and asking how young bone marrow changes the ageing brain.

My placement. I joined this project in September 2026 as an undergraduate research assistant.

What I'm thinking about. In mid-2026, two studies reported that from around age 50, microglia in the human brain are gradually replaced by cells made in the bone marrow – by about 80, almost all of them (Alzforum, Aug 2026). Florent Ginhoux called the blood system "a previously underappreciated contributor" to microglial biology. If that is right, the quality of what the bone marrow makes matters for the ageing brain. A month later, Michal Schwartz's group showed that in amyloid mice, chronic type I interferon disrupts monocyte production in the marrow, and that blocking it protected neurons (Alzforum, Sep 2026). Put next to the Arimura lab's Down syndrome result, I see a loop: the diseased brain may reshape the marrow, and the marrow can feed back on the brain. Nathan Zemke rightly calls this "an emerging concept rather than an established paradigm" – which is exactly what makes it exciting to work on.

Organelle-contact abnormalities in neurodegenerative disease

Shimane University · Faculty of Medicine, Department of Life Science (Chemistry) · Jun – Aug 2026 · Izumo, Japan

Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Seiji Watanabe

The question. In neurodegeneration, protein build-up, failing mitochondria and inflammation all seem to feed each other – but where do they meet? One candidate is the mitochondria-associated membrane (MAM), the contact site between the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria that handles Ca2+ transfer, lipid synthesis, mitochondrial dynamics, autophagy and responses to proteostatic stress.

The host lab. Assoc. Prof. Watanabe and colleagues have shown that MAM disruption is shared across ALS-causing genes, and that MAM collapse impairs the TBK1-mediated proteostatic stress response. The lab now asks which MAM sub-domains are selectively damaged in disease, whether microglia also show MAM abnormalities, and whether restoring the MAM can calm neuroinflammation.

My placement. My project, Investigation of novel organelle-contact abnormalities using neurodegenerative disease models, was planned to compare MAM formation, localisation and related molecules between disease models and controls using the lab's established cell systems and stored samples, and to relate them to mitochondrial morphology, proteostatic stress and neuroinflammation.

What I'm thinking about. In August 2026, researchers described "mitochondrial plaques" – huge clusters of mitochondria-packed dystrophic neurites next to amyloid plaques in mouse models and in Alzheimer's brain (Alzforum, Aug 2026). Ralph Nixon read them as one facet of a wider collapse of autophagy in vulnerable neurons. That made me look at the problem from the other end: the MAM is one of the places where mitochondria are divided, quality-checked and handed to autophagy. Could contact-site failure sit upstream of that pile-up? The idea is not new – back in 2014, TDP-43 was already shown to loosen the ER–mitochondria bond (Alzforum, Jun 2014) – but the tools to watch contacts in tissue are only now catching up. I find it a lovely shift in perspective: from asking which organelle breaks to asking where organelles stop talking.

Ageing-induced senoinflammation in dementia

Institute of Science Tokyo · Graduate School of Medical and Dental Sciences, Department of Pharmacology · Jan – Feb 2026 · Tokyo, Japan

Supervisor: Prof. Tomoyuki Furuyashiki

The question. Why does age make the brain vulnerable? One answer is senescent cells – cells that stop dividing but don't die, and keep releasing inflammatory signals ("senoinflammation") into their surroundings.

The host lab. Prof. Furuyashiki's laboratory studies how ageing and chronic stress reshape brain circuits through inflammation, using mouse models of stress- and age-driven inflammation and linking them to immune markers in patients. Recent work found that synaptic mitochondrial oxidative stress in the prefrontal cortex helps explain why some aged mice lose cognitive flexibility and others don't. The lab is part of the Moonshot Goal 7 Higuchi Project, which aims to detect and control dementia early, based on the idea that glial pathology develops into senoinflammation.

My placement. I trained in the lab on this theme in January–February 2026.

What I'm thinking about. Senolytics – drugs that clear senescent cells – have been tested in small Alzheimer's studies: dasatinib reached the cerebrospinal fluid, but biomarkers and cognition did not change over 12 weeks; a larger placebo-controlled trial is expected to run into 2027 (Alzforum therapeutics database). Meanwhile, Li Gan's group found that senescent microglia in a mouse model of ageing release one specific factor, DLK1, that disrupts myelin and neuronal signalling (Alzforum, Aug 2026). Rahul Nagvekar noted that myelin breakdown has long been linked to neurodegeneration but rarely targeted, and this names a candidate driver. To me, this suggests a gentler strategy: instead of removing senescent cells, find and silence their most harmful messages. That also makes the Moonshot goal of visualising senoinflammation feel essential – we can only target what we can see.

The role of MARK4 in neuroinflammation

Tokyo Metropolitan University · Graduate School of Science, Laboratory of Molecular Neuroscience · Dec 2025 – Jan 2026 · Tokyo, Japan

Supervisor: Prof. Kanae Ando

The question. Tau turns toxic long before it forms tangles. Which enzymes push it there, and can we stop them?

The host lab. Prof. Ando's laboratory studies MARK4, a kinase genetically and pathologically linked to Alzheimer's disease. The lab has shown that deleting Mark4 in a tauopathy mouse model reduces pathological tau, neurodegeneration and astrogliosis and improves memory and survival, and has developed a cell-penetrating peptide that blocks MARK4 and eases tau toxicity – work aimed at new drugs. The lab also studies how neuronal and glial metabolism shape brain ageing.

My placement. I studied the role of MARK4 in neuroinflammation, supported by an Undergraduate Research Scholarship from Tokyo Metropolitan University.

What I'm thinking about. In 2025, Thomas Karikari's group characterised the soluble tau assemblies that form before tangles: their core carries phosphorylation at serines 262 and 356, and these species in cerebrospinal fluid tracked tangle load and cognitive decline (Alzforum, Feb 2025). Those are exactly the sites MARK-family kinases target. Yet the Ando lab's own fly work adds an important twist: MARK1, 2 and 4 all raised phosphorylation at these sites, but only MARK4 caused tau to accumulate – so the sites alone don't explain toxicity. Years earlier, blocking Nuak1, another AMPK-related kinase acting at Ser356, also reduced tau pathology in mice, and Bingwei Lu suggested this kinase family could offer a new way into tauopathies (Alzforum, Oct 2016). I'm curious whether the same early tau species could serve as both a warning sign and a readout for MARK4-targeted therapies.
