How does an ageing body push the brain toward dementia, and where could we step in?

Dementia is one of the greatest health challenges of our ageing societies, yet the processes that drive the brain from healthy ageing into neurodegeneration remain poorly understood. I am interested in how the brain's clearance, immune and organelle systems break down with age, how the rest of the body – especially the bone marrow and the immune cells it makes – shapes that failure, and how these failures can become targets for new medicines. Since 2025 I have trained in four laboratories in Japan, each approaching the question from a different side.

  • Bone marrow and the brainTohoku University
  • Where organelles meetShimane University
  • Ageing and senoinflammationInstitute of Science Tokyo
  • MARK4 and early tauTokyo Metropolitan University

Research interests

  • Microglia
  • Neuroinflammation
  • Brain–bone marrow axis
  • Neurovascular unit
  • Tau pathology
  • Brain clearance
  • Biomarkers
  • Drug discovery
  • Brain–bone marrow axis – how ageing and disease reshape blood formation in the bone marrow (including the skull marrow next to the brain), how this changes the immune cells that reach the brain, and whether resetting this "immune cell factory" could protect it.
  • Glia and the neurovascular unit – how microglia, astrocytes, pericytes and blood vessels work with neurons, and how their interactions drive neuroinflammation, amyloid-β and tau pathology and cognitive decline.
  • Proteostasis and brain clearance – how disease proteins such as amyloid-β and tau are made, modified and accumulate, and how they are removed by protein degradation, the glymphatic system and meningeal and vascular routes.
  • From mechanism to medicine – turning these mechanisms into early biomarkers and new therapies, such as glia-targeted treatments, protection of the neurovascular unit and cell-based approaches.

Tohoku University, Sendai, Japan. Sep 2026 – present

Could dementia begin, in part, in the bone marrow?

Haematopoietic stem cells and the origins of dementia
Laboratory of Pharmacology, Graduate School of Pharmaceutical Sciences. Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Nariko Arimura

In the host lab. The earliest triggers of dementia are still unknown, and the blood – its cells and everything they secrete – is emerging as a surprisingly strong influence on the brain. Assoc. Prof. Arimura's laboratory studies how changes in haematopoietic (blood-forming) stem cells shape brain function. People with Down syndrome almost all develop early-onset Alzheimer's disease, and the lab has found that wild-type mice given bone marrow from a Down syndrome model develop cognitive decline even though their brains are genetically normal. The team is now tracking the plasma metabolites and exosomes from these blood cells that reach the brain, and asking how young bone marrow changes the ageing brain.

My placement. I joined this project in September 2026 as an undergraduate research assistant.

What I'm thinking about

In mid-2026, two studies reported that from around age 50, microglia in the human brain are gradually replaced by cells made in the bone marrow – by about 80, almost all of them (Alzforum, Aug 2026). Florent Ginhoux called the blood system "a previously underappreciated contributor" to microglial biology. If that is right, the quality of what the bone marrow makes matters for the ageing brain. A month later, Michal Schwartz's group showed that in amyloid mice, chronic type I interferon disrupts monocyte production in the marrow, and that blocking it protected neurons (Alzforum, Sep 2026). Put next to the Arimura lab's Down syndrome result, I see a loop: the diseased brain may reshape the marrow, and the marrow can feed back on the brain. Nathan Zemke rightly calls this "an emerging concept rather than an established paradigm" – which is exactly what makes it exciting to work on.

Shimane University, Izumo, Japan. Jun – Aug 2026

Where do protein build-up, failing mitochondria and inflammation meet?

Organelle-contact abnormalities in neurodegenerative disease
Department of Life Science (Chemistry), Faculty of Medicine. Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Seiji Watanabe

In the host lab. One candidate meeting point is the mitochondria-associated membrane (MAM), the contact site between the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria that handles Ca2+ transfer, lipid synthesis, mitochondrial dynamics, autophagy and responses to proteostatic stress. Assoc. Prof. Watanabe and colleagues have shown that MAM disruption is shared across ALS-causing genes, and that MAM collapse impairs the TBK1-mediated proteostatic stress response. The lab now asks which MAM sub-domains are selectively damaged in disease, whether microglia also show MAM abnormalities, and whether restoring the MAM can calm neuroinflammation.

My placement. My project, Investigation of novel organelle-contact abnormalities using neurodegenerative disease models, was planned to compare MAM formation, localisation and related molecules between disease models and controls using the lab's established cell systems and stored samples, and to relate them to mitochondrial morphology, proteostatic stress and neuroinflammation.

What I'm thinking about

In August 2026, researchers described "mitochondrial plaques" – huge clusters of mitochondria-packed dystrophic neurites next to amyloid plaques in mouse models and in Alzheimer's brain (Alzforum, Aug 2026). Ralph Nixon read them as one facet of a wider collapse of autophagy in vulnerable neurons. That made me look at the problem from the other end: the MAM is one of the places where mitochondria are divided, quality-checked and handed to autophagy. Could contact-site failure sit upstream of that pile-up? The idea is not new – back in 2014, TDP-43 was already shown to loosen the ER–mitochondria bond (Alzforum, Jun 2014) – but the tools to watch contacts in tissue are only now catching up. I find it a lovely shift in perspective: from asking which organelle breaks to asking where organelles stop talking.

Institute of Science Tokyo, Tokyo, Japan. Jan – Feb 2026

Why does age make the brain so vulnerable?

Ageing-induced senoinflammation in dementia
Department of Pharmacology, Graduate School of Medical and Dental Sciences. Supervisor: Prof. Tomoyuki Furuyashiki

In the host lab. One answer is senescent cells – cells that stop dividing but don't die, and keep releasing inflammatory signals ("senoinflammation") into their surroundings. Prof. Furuyashiki's laboratory studies how ageing and chronic stress reshape brain circuits through inflammation in the brain and the periphery. The lab has shown that long-term stress mobilises blood cells from the bone marrow into the brain, where they contribute to behavioural change – the same body-to-brain route that runs through my current project. Recent work found that synaptic mitochondrial oxidative stress in the prefrontal cortex helps explain why some aged mice lose cognitive flexibility and others don't. The lab is part of two Moonshot Goal 7 projects on preventing and controlling dementia.

My placement. I trained in the lab on this theme in January–February 2026.

What I'm thinking about

Senolytics – drugs that clear senescent cells – have been tested in small Alzheimer's studies: dasatinib reached the cerebrospinal fluid, but biomarkers and cognition did not change over 12 weeks; a larger placebo-controlled trial is expected to run into 2027 (Alzforum therapeutics database). Meanwhile, Li Gan's group found that senescent microglia in a mouse model of ageing release one specific factor, DLK1, that disrupts myelin and neuronal signalling (Alzforum, Aug 2026). Rahul Nagvekar noted that myelin breakdown has long been linked to neurodegeneration but rarely targeted, and this names a candidate driver. To me, this suggests a gentler strategy: instead of removing senescent cells, find and silence their most harmful messages. That also makes the Moonshot goal of visualising senoinflammation feel essential – we can only target what we can see.

Host-lab projects: AMED Moonshot Goal 7, Higuchi Project (PM: Dr Makoto Higuchi, QST; Prof. Furuyashiki is a project member) – Early detection and control of dementia pathogenesis based on the concept of glial pathology developing into senoinflammation; AMED Moonshot Goal 7, Isa Project (Prof. Furuyashiki is a project member) – Brain reserve functions that support resilience against dementia, and ways to strengthen them; AMED Brain/MINDS 2.0 3B-05-R7, FY2025–2029 (PI: Prof. Furuyashiki) – Whole-brain neural-circuit changes originating from inflammation in psychiatric and neurological disorders.

Tokyo Metropolitan University, Tokyo, Japan. Dec 2025 – Jan 2026

Which enzymes push tau toward toxicity, and can we stop them?

The role of MARK4 in neuroinflammation
Laboratory of Molecular Neuroscience, Graduate School of Science. Supervisor: Prof. Kanae Ando

In the host lab. Tau turns toxic long before it forms tangles. Prof. Ando's laboratory studies MARK4, a kinase genetically and pathologically linked to Alzheimer's disease. The lab has shown that deleting Mark4 in a tauopathy mouse model reduces pathological tau, neurodegeneration and astrogliosis and improves memory and survival, and has developed a cell-penetrating peptide that blocks MARK4 and eases tau toxicity – work aimed at new drugs. The lab also studies how neuronal and glial metabolism shape brain ageing; in a fly model of tau toxicity, boosting glucose uptake in glia calmed neuroinflammation and protected neurons.

My placement. I studied the role of MARK4 in neuroinflammation, supported by an Undergraduate Research Scholarship from Tokyo Metropolitan University.

What I'm thinking about

In 2025, Thomas Karikari's group characterised the soluble tau assemblies that form before tangles: their core carries phosphorylation at serines 262 and 356, and these species in cerebrospinal fluid tracked tangle load and cognitive decline (Alzforum, Feb 2025). Those are exactly the sites MARK-family kinases target. Yet the Ando lab's own fly work adds an important twist: MARK1, 2 and 4 all raised phosphorylation at these sites, but only MARK4 caused tau to accumulate – so the sites alone don't explain toxicity. Years earlier, blocking Nuak1, another AMPK-related kinase acting at Ser356, also reduced tau pathology in mice, and Bingwei Lu suggested this kinase family could offer a new way into tauopathies (Alzforum, Oct 2016). I'm curious whether the same early tau species could serve as both a warning sign and a readout for MARK4-targeted therapies.
