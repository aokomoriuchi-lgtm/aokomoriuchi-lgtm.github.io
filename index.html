Welcome to my website!

My name is Ziyao Qing. I am a Dean's Scholar in the Bachelor of Pharmaceutical Science Advanced (Honours) Scholars Program at the Monash Institute of Pharmaceutical Sciences (MIPS), Monash University, Melbourne.

I am passionate about understanding why the ageing brain becomes vulnerable to dementia, and about turning that knowledge into new medicines. My interests sit at the interface of neuroscience and pharmacology, with a particular focus on microglia and neuroinflammation, tau pathology, the brain's clearance systems and, increasingly, the dialogue between the brain and the bone marrow that supplies its immune cells.

In 2021, an estimated 57 million people worldwide were living with dementia (WHO), yet treatments that truly change the course of the disease remain scarce. I believe dementia is not only a disease of the brain. Understanding how the brain's clearance systems, immune responses and cellular organelles fail with age – and how the rest of the body, especially the blood-forming system, feeds into that failure – will reveal new routes to therapies that can slow or prevent neurodegeneration.

This is the question I keep returning to while working on haematopoietic stem cells in the Arimura laboratory: what if part of what goes wrong in the ageing brain starts in the bone marrow? Recent work from Michal Schwartz's group (Abellanas et al., Nature Neuroscience 2026) found that in an amyloid mouse model, chronic type I interferon signalling pushes haematopoietic stem cells out of quiescence and impairs monocyte production, limiting the supply of protective monocyte-derived macrophages to the brain; blocking this signal restored the monocyte supply and protected neurons, and blood monocytes from people with Alzheimer's disease showed a similar signature. Alongside the Arimura laboratory's observation that bone marrow from a Down syndrome model can impair cognition in mice whose brains are genetically normal, this points to a two-way loop: signals from the diseased brain may reshape the immune "factory" in the bone marrow, and an altered bone marrow can in turn feed back on the brain. I want to understand how this loop is set up with age, whether it can be read out in blood, and whether it can be reset – keeping in mind that it is still an emerging idea built largely on mouse models.

To pursue this, I have trained in four research laboratories across Japan, each tackling neurodegeneration from a different angle – from the Alzheimer's-related kinase MARK4 and ageing-induced inflammation, to communication between organelles and the contribution of blood stem cells to dementia.
